How to choose a product development firm comes down to one test: can they show you a product they took from concept all the way to manufacturing? A portfolio of renderings is easy to put together, and shipping a product is much harder. Below is the scorecard we would use, what firms charge in 2026, the contract terms that protect you and the questions that separate a real engineering partner from a design studio.

## What should you look for in a product development firm?

Look for proof of shipped products, an engineering process you can see, a contract that hands you everything and a quote with its assumptions written down, roughly in that order of importance. Price matters, but it is the easiest thing to compare and the least useful on its own.

We weight the criteria like this. The weights are our judgment from building hardware and software products, not an industry standard, so move them if your product is unusual.

| Criterion | Weight | What a strong answer looks like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shipped products like yours | 30% | Names a product they took through tooling and a pilot run, and lets you speak to that client |
| Process and testing | 25% | Clear stages with decision gates, a separate test team, code under source control you can access |
| IP and handover terms | 20% | You own the design, firmware, board files and computer-aided design (CAD) files outright, with editable source files delivered |
| Quote quality | 15% | Says what is included, how many revisions, what triggers a change order and how parts are billed |
| Rate and location | 10% | A rate that fits the budget, with the tax effect of location counted (see below) |

Score each firm from 1 to 5 on every row, multiply by the weight and add it up. If two firms land within a few points of each other, pick the one whose engineers you would rather argue with for a year.

## Ask for the product, not the portfolio

The single best question comes from [Unbox Product Design](https://www.unboxpd.com/post/how-to-choose-the-right-product-development-firm), a San Francisco firm. Ask them: "Show me a product you helped take from concept through manufacturing." The answer tells you more than any set of concept renderings, because most of the money and risk sits after the pretty pictures.

Then check who will do the work. Sprout, a product design firm, points out that the team in the sales meeting is not always the team on your project. Ask for the names of the lead engineer and the program manager, and whether firmware and electrical work happen in house or go to a subcontractor. Cad Crowd notes that a design firm without an electrical engineer has to outsource that part.

Cardinal Peak adds two quick checks. Ask how many people are on their quality assurance (QA) team, and walk away if the answer is that engineers test their own code. Ask what share of their work comes from repeat clients. Firms that ship good products get hired again.

## How much do product development firms charge?

Most US design firms charge from $100 to more than $200 an hour, according to [Cad Crowd](https://www.cadcrowd.com/blog/cost-estimation-for-an-electronic-product-design-services-rates-and-pricing-for-your-company/), and some offshore firms advertise $20 to $25. Cad Crowd's explanation for the gap is speed: an experienced team can finish in far fewer hours.

RMBG compiled 2026 rates for a senior embedded engineer: $120 to $180 an hour in the US, $90 to $130 in Western Europe and $40 to $70 in India, Vietnam or the Philippines. Unbox publishes planning ranges by stage, which help you sanity check the total.

| Stage | Typical timeline | Typical investment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Proof of concept or feasibility | 1 to 3 months | $10,000 to $40,000+ |
| Industrial design | 3 to 6 months | $20,000 to $100,000+ |
| Product engineering | 6 months to 1 year | $20,000 to $300,000+ |
| Prototyping and testing | 1 to 3 months | $10,000 to $100,000+ |
| Manufacturing setup | 6 months to 1 year | $30,000 to $500,000+ |

Those are Unbox's ranges, and it warns that complex products can go well past them. For a line by line view of a first device, see our guide to [hardware prototype cost](/blog/hardware-prototype-cost).

## Does an offshore firm really save money?

Sometimes, but a US company should count the tax effect before comparing hourly rates. Since the One Big Beautiful Bill Act of July 2025, research done in the US can be deducted in the year you pay for it under the new Section 174A. Research done abroad still has to be spread over 15 years, starting from the middle of the year it is paid, as [EY](https://taxnews.ey.com/news/2025-1432-tax-reconciliation-legislation-significantly-affects-cost-recovery-and-accounting-method-provisions) explains.

Here is the worked example. Say you spend $200,000 on engineering this year.

- With a US firm, the full $200,000 is deductible this year.
- With an offshore firm, you deduct $200,000 divided by 15, then halved for the midpoint rule: about $6,667 this year, and $13,333 a year after that.

That is a timing difference, and your accountant should run it on your own numbers. RMBG puts it more bluntly: offshore work gives up most US research tax relief. A rate that is half the price can still be the cheaper project, but the gap is smaller than the rate card suggests.

The UK has a similar rule for accounting periods starting on or after 1 April 2024. [Guidance from HM Revenue and Customs (HMRC)](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/corporate-intangibles-research-and-development-manual/cird150500) says payments to contractors for research done outside the UK generally do not qualify for R&D relief. Lower cost or easier hiring abroad does not count as a reason for an exception.

## Who owns the design when the project ends?

You should, but only if the contract says so. Under US copyright law, work by an outside firm is a "work made for hire" only if it fits one of nine listed categories and both sides sign a written agreement saying so, according to the [U.S. Copyright Office](https://www.copyright.gov/circs/circ30.pdf). Firmware, circuit board files and CAD models usually do not fit those categories, so the contract needs a clause that assigns the rights to you. Have your lawyer check it.

Cardinal Peak warns about firms that keep a "predeveloped" piece of the design and use it to keep you tied to them. Unbox's list of manufacturing questions adds who owns the tooling, who controls the CAD and production documents, and whether the product can move to another factory if the first one fails.

## Red flags and questions to ask before you sign

Unbox's red flags are worth reading in full. The ones we see most often are unrealistically low estimates, promises of extremely fast development, vague scopes of work, no defined testing plan and handing designs straight to a factory to engineer. A firm that cannot explain what happens after the prototype has probably not been there.

Send every shortlisted firm the same brief and ask the same questions:

```steps
["Show us a product you took from concept through manufacturing, and let us call that client.", "Who will lead our project, and which work is done in house versus by subcontractors?", "How many people are on your test team, and can we see the code repository during the project?", "What does this quote assume: which stages, how many prototype revisions, and what triggers a change order?", "Will we own the design, firmware, board files and CAD, with editable source files delivered at each stage?", "Who owns the tooling, and can production move to another factory if needed?"]
```

On pricing, Cad Crowd suggests paying hourly during early design and switching to a fixed price once the scope is defined. Sprout makes the related point that the billing model matters less than what the estimate assumes.

## How we would approach this

If you asked us to quote, we would first check what is true about the idea: what the first prototype has to prove, what can be bought off the shelf, and what has to be designed. We only quote for work we can deliver ourselves, and we will say so when a module or an existing product makes a custom design unnecessary. The same team handles hardware, firmware and the software around them. Every stage ends with editable source files and documentation in your hands, so you could take the next stage elsewhere.
