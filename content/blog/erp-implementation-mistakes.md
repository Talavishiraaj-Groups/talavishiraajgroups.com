The ERP implementation mistakes that hurt most are rarely about the software. They are unclear goals, messy data, rushed timelines, too much customization and too little preparation of the people who will use the system. If you are about to sign with a vendor, or you are halfway through a rollout that feels shaky, this is the list to check against.

## How often do ERP projects go wrong?

Often enough to plan for it, though the numbers depend on who you ask and when. Panorama Consulting's [2015 report](https://www.panorama-consulting.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/07/2015-ERP-Report-3.pdf) found that 55% of projects in 2014 ran over budget and 75% ran over schedule, and that 41% of organizations got less than half the benefits they expected.

Its [2026 report](https://hs.panorama-consulting.com/hubfs/Reports/ERP%20Report/2026-erp-report-panorama-consulting-group.pdf) looks better: more than a quarter of respondents were over budget and almost a quarter were over schedule. The two surveys used different samples (the 2026 group had a median annual revenue of $200.5 million and 56.5% were multinational), so read them as two snapshots and not as a trend. Panorama also sells ERP consulting, which is worth remembering when you read its findings.

## What are the most common ERP implementation mistakes?

There are eight that show up again and again across Prosci's research and Panorama's surveys. The table pairs each with a fix you can act on this month.

| Mistake | What it looks like | What to do instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Vague goals | "Reduce inefficiency" with no number | Pick 3 to 5 measurable targets, such as days to close the books or order errors per month |
| Dirty data | Duplicate customers, wrong bills of material | Assign a data owner and clean the data before migration, not after |
| Rushed timeline | A go-live date picked before scoping | Build the plan from module count and team capacity, then set the date |
| Heavy customization | Rebuilding the old system inside the new one | Adopt standard workflows first and customize only where the business truly differs |
| Thin testing | Testers who are not the daily users | Have the people who run the process test it with real data |
| Weak change management | One training session the week before go-live | Start in month one, with a named sponsor and regular updates |
| Wrong fit | A system built for a bigger or smaller company | Match the system to your size, number of sites and growth plan |
| No plan for after go-live | The team disbands the day the system goes live | Budget for support, fixes and a 3 and 6 month review |

[Prosci lists most of these](https://www.prosci.com/blog/why-do-erp-implementations-fail) as causes of failure, and its guidance on customization is plain: replicating legacy processes in a new system adds cost, complexity and a long-term maintenance burden.

## Why do ERP projects run over budget and schedule?

Budget overruns usually come from surprises in scope, and schedule overruns usually come from people. In Panorama's 2026 survey, the top reason for going over budget was an unexpected need for additional technology. The top reason for going over schedule was organizational issues, such as governance gaps, resistance to change and arguments over process redesign.

Panorama also reports that fewer than a quarter of organizations said they put an intense focus on change management. That fits the schedule finding. Delayed sign-offs and long design workshops are what resistance looks like in a project plan.

A rough test for any plan: if the schedule has a line for configuration and no line for training, data cleanup or process sign-off, the date is a guess.

## Should you replace your ERP or fix how you use it?

Check how well you use the current system before you replace it. IMEC, an advisory group for manufacturers, wrote in [May 2026](https://www.imec.org/news/erp-failure-is-usually-not-a-software-problem/) that many manufacturers swap out a capable ERP and find the same problems in the new one. Its examples: inaccurate lead times, poor bill of material discipline, spreadsheet workarounds and no clear process ownership.

IMEC suggests optimization as a first step, including cleaning the data, standardizing planning, improving routing accuracy and setting up a cross-functional governance team. If the same issues remain after that, the software may be the limit, and replacement is easier to justify. ## What should you check before you sign?

Run these six checks before you sign a contract or approve the next phase of a rollout. They take a few days, and each one catches a mistake from the table above.

```steps
["Write the 3 to 5 business numbers the ERP must move, with today's baseline for each.", "Ask who owns each data set (customers, items, suppliers, prices) and how clean it is today.", "Ask the vendor for a month by month plan that shows data migration, testing, training and post go-live support as separate lines.", "List every customization you are asking for and challenge each one: can the standard workflow do this?", "Name the executive sponsor and the people from each department who will test with real data.", "Agree dates for a 3 month and 6 month review against the numbers from step one."]
```

If the vendor cannot answer step three, that tells you something about the project you are about to buy. Treat a vague answer as a reason to slow down. It costs far less to spend two more weeks on the plan than to extend a rollout by two months because nobody scoped the data work.
