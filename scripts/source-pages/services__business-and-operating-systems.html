Business systems and operating design

We work out what is actually limiting the business, then design the operating model, workflows and decision structures that remove the limit.

Organizations that grew quickly usually run on arrangements that were never designed, only accumulated. That works until coordination cost grows faster than revenue. The work here is establishing what is genuinely in the way, then designing and building the operating structure that removes it rather than documenting the current mess more thoroughly.

Signals you would recognize

  • Growth is arriving faster than the operating model can absorb it
  • Approvals, exceptions and status tracking still route through one or two people
  • Nobody can say, with evidence, which bottleneck matters most
  • Process documentation exists and nobody follows it

When to consider it

Consider this when the business is growing but coordination cost is growing faster, or when a major technology or hiring decision is pending and the operating picture is not clear enough to commit.

What we do

  • Analysis across strategy, operations, people, technology and data
  • Operating model and decision rights design
  • End to end workflow mapping, redesign and documentation
  • Reporting and operational visibility, built on real data rather than manual collation
  • Governance, escalation and risk frameworks for regulated or enterprise buyers

How we approach it

01

Establish the facts before the recommendations

We work from interviews, workflow evidence and system data rather than from an opinion formed in the first meeting. What people describe and what the systems record often differ, and the gap is usually where the problem is.

02

Design for the team that exists

An operating model that requires headcount or discipline the organization does not have is a document, not a change. We design against actual capacity.

03

Build what the design requires

Most operating changes need something engineered: a reporting layer, an integration, an automation. We build it rather than hand over a specification.

What you end up with

  • A clear account of what is limiting the business, with the evidence behind it
  • Current state and target state workflow maps
  • A sequenced plan with owners, dependencies and effort
  • The reporting and systems changes that make the design operable

Related work

  • Giving a founder a single view of operations

    Operations scattered across spreadsheets and chat threads were consolidated into one view of work, risks and next actions.

  • Automating decision-heavy internal workflows

    Manual expert decisions were mapped, codified and selectively automated without losing human control.

  • Designing a structured sales hiring engine

    High-volume, commission-based hiring was treated as a measurable funnel with explicit stages and rubrics.

Questions we are asked

How is this different from management consulting?
We do the consulting and then build what we recommend, in the same engagement. An operating design almost always depends on something being engineered, whether that is a reporting layer, an integration or an automation, so a recommendation that stops at the specification stalls at exactly that point. Management consulting is a different purchase and a legitimate one: it addresses organisational questions spanning several functions, and execution is somebody else’s problem by design. If your question is organisational rather than technical, that is the better route and we will say so.
We already have process documentation nobody follows. Why would this be different?
Because documentation that nobody follows is usually a symptom rather than the problem. A process is ignored when it requires attention, headcount or discipline the organisation does not actually have, so the work is to design against real capacity rather than to write the same process down more thoroughly. Where a step is only followed because one person remembers to do it, the answer is normally to build it into a system rather than to restate it in a document.
What if we already know what the problem is?
Then say so, and if you are right this is the wrong engagement to buy. Sometimes the named problem really is the thing holding the business back, in which case what you need is it built rather than an analysis telling you what you already knew. The reason it is worth checking first is that the loudest complaint and the actual constraint are often different, and the way to tell them apart is whether what people describe matches what the systems record. Where those two agree, we will say the analysis is unnecessary and move to the build.
